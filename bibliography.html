Annotated Bibilography

This annotated bibliography, which includes seminal theoretical texts on social art history, can help facilitate a more thorough introduction to the theory and practice.

  • Baxandall, Michael. “Conditions of Trade” and “The Period Eye,” in Painting and Experience in Fifteenth Century Italy: A Primer in the Social History of Pictorial Style (London and New York: Oxford University Press, 1974), 1–108.
    • "Conditions of Trade" Baxandall foregrounds the economic relationship between client and artist as an inherent feature of fifteenth-century painting. “Money,” he declares, “is very important in the history of art” (1). This client/artist relationship most often materialized through legal contracts, where the client (to varying degrees of specificity) dictated their desires to the painter, including details regarding form (like specific pigments/colors) and content. Through an analysis of such contracts, Baxandall highlights the economics of painting—the cost of materials, skill, and labor under commercial capitalism—and what these factors signify as important and how they are represented in the finished product.
    • "The period eye" In Baxandall’s chapter on The period eye, he argues that 15th century painting was made for viewers whose learned habits of seeing shaped what the painting meant and how it was designed. Baxandall explains that vision is not purely natural, but as our eyes receive light, the mind interprets it through learned categories, experiences, and pattern recognition. When looking at Renaissance painting, Quattrocento viewers could notice and value features in paintings differently than contemporary viewers. Baxandall defines the period eye as the shared visual equipment, rather than the viewer’s private response. Quattrocento viewers of art were trained to see through religious practice, gesture & movement, social performance and scientific tools (geometry, proportion, perspective, etc). Through social conditioning of the time, viewers of art in the 15th century were able to read subtle differences in emotion and storytelling. Baxandall posits that Renaissance painting should be understood as interactions between an artists’ visual design and a historically specific viewer equipped with religious, scientific, and socially specific knowledge.
  • Clark, T. J. “On the Social History of Art,” in Modern Art and Modernism: A Critical Anthology, eds. Francis Frascina and Charles Harrison (Boulder, CO: Westview Press, 1987), 249-258.
    • In this seminole essay, Clark argues that how art historians have treated the subject in the past has been woefully ignorant of how histories and art intersect. Clark is interested in the how and less concerned with “analogies between form and content.” He posits that French art in the mid-19th century was inextricably linked to politics and this essay concerns this specific moment in French art, aiming to explore the complex links between the two fields. Clark believes that art historians do not practice social art history, as they view history as a fixed and linear story that is essentially applicable to every artwork. Social art history is the process of conversion and relation, aiming to discover the real network of relations between form and content. Clark examines popular waves of French art including avant-garde, Realism, Individualism and popular art. Clark ultimately rejects the idea that artworks merely “reflect” ideologies or historical events, and advances a more holistic theory of social art history that accounts for the artist as a discursive agent within a larger social and economic structure, of which the artist can respond accordingly. This broadens the concept beyond generalized analyses of class and ideology and their correlation to works of art, forging historically and socially specific modes of inquiry.
  • Clark, T. J. “Olympia’s Choice,” in The Painting of Modern Life: Paris in the Art of Manet and His Followers, rev. ed. (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1984), 79-146.
    • In this essay, Clark dissects the social art history of Manet's Olympia (1863-65). Clark argues that Olympia was scandalous because Manet exposed uncomfortable links among sexuality, class and money in 'modern' Paris. Olympia reveals the state of affairs and stakes advantage of our assumptions as spectators. The work is not only a provocative nude, but her direct gaze disrupts conventional ways of seeing women and the nude genre. Clark explores the formal and social connections that made the piece so controversial, examining several reviews of the work from the 1865 Salon. The response to the work emphasized Manet's supposed critique of Parisian society and points to the evidence of what the painting made visible that was uncomfortable to acknowledge. Prostitution was both highly visible and heavily regulated and became a symbol through which people expressed anxieties about urban development, class, disease (especially sexual disease), morality and the commodification of power. Before this painting deputed at the 1865 Salon, female nudes were only depicted through myth and allegory and were definitively meant to be un-sexed. Clark posits that depictions of the nude were torturously teetering between desire and observation and artists before Manet tried their best to show that "Desire is no part of the nude: the nude is human form in general, abstracted from life, attraction, even gender." (pg 128) The essence of Olympia's modernity is what scandalized her, threatening the myth of the courtesan, the nude genre and the delicate balance between body and class.
  • Craven, David, "Marxism and Critical Art History," in A Companion to Art Theory, ed. Paul Smith and Carolyn Wilde (Malden, MA: Blackwell Publishing, 2002), 267-283.
    • David Craven’s chapter examines some common misconceptions of Marxist theory, spanning class, ideology, and the state. This chapter is primarily focused on defining these central theoretical terms, not just in relation to art history. One of the key concepts that Craven promotes is a historical materialist understanding of art: art does not passively reflect class, ideological, or state interests, but actively contributes to the construction of historical reality. He also refutes the orthodox Marxist approach which presupposes grand universal socioeconomic superstructures as being central to the history of art, arguing instead for culturally specific analyses of art and its production and reception. The chapter ends briefly with a genealogy of social art history, starting with its early pioneers in the twentieth century and ending with the leading contemporary scholars working in the field.
  • Munro, Thomas. “The Marxist Theory of Art History: Socio-Economic Determinism and the Dialectical Process.” The Journal of Aesthetics and Art Criticism, vol. 18, no. 4 (1960): 430–45. https://doi.org/10.2307/428109.
    • This essay examines the Marxist theory of social art history, emphasizing socio-economic factors, dialectical processes, and the influence of Marxist and other social theories on interpreting art styles in Western art. Art and culture were seen by Marxists as superstructures shaped by underlying socio-economic struggles, reflecting class interests and general economic conditions. Class stratification is the primary cause of style and formalism, and in turn, cultural and historical change. Art is a social reflection and is meant to reinforce or challenge existing social power structures. Marxist social art history also links formalist qualities with class, creating a direct correlation between how art looks and the class of the artist. For Marxists, art was used to enforce and support communist ideology, and thus creating a value system within art that was used to reiterate Soviet values. Abstraction and experimentation were seen as frivolous and upper class, whereas realism and compositional simplicity were tools of the proletariat.
  • Sekula, Allan, "The Traffic in Photographs," Art Journal 41, no. 1 (1981): 15-25.
    • Allan Sekula’s article analyzes dominant photographic discourses that claim photographs as a pure art or science while obscuring the “traffic” of photographs, that is their production, circulation, inscription, and commodification. Central to Sekula’s argument is the idea that photography emerged as an extension of a bourgeois ideological apparatus that treated the meaning of photographs, through formal analysis and pseudo-scientific rationalism, as a universal language of its own. Sekula cites as an example the photo exhibition The Family of Man, which, under the veil of liberal humanism, conceals the specific political and commercial interests strategically aligned with the exhibition that made its international circuit possible. Photography, Sekula argues, is necessarily subject to greater material and discursive forces beyond the individual “genius” of the artist.
  • Wilson, Kristina, "'The Quick Appraising Glance': Decorative Accessories and the Staged Self," in Midcentury-Modernism and the American Body: Race, Gender, and the Politics of Power in Design (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2021), 161-213.
    • Kristina Willson’s chapter analyzes mid-twentieth-century decorative arts in American households and their social function as symbols of taste and personality. This chapter belongs to Willson’s larger monograph on American modernist design and the body. Through an analysis of form and cultural discourse (like advertisements and magazine articles), Willson posits that such designs — ranging from cocktail glasses to quasi-African masks — embody gendered and racial ideologies that are commodified in a particular post-war white suburban milieu. Rather than challenging socioeconomic superstructures or advancing the utilitarian ideal of modernist design, these objects, Willson argues, underscore the presence of a culture of consumption based on judgement and self-identity.
