John Singer Sargent, Madame X, 1884, oil on canvas, 92.5 × 43.25 in. "American Prestige," designed by Edwin Fuerst, manufactured by Libbey Glass Company, 1938

John Singer Sargent’s Madame X (1884) is a nearly life-size full body portrait of Parisian socialite and iconic party-girl, Virginie Amélie Avegno Gautreau. Dressed in a floor-length black gown with a sweetheart neckline held up by two gold straps on her shockingly pale shoulders, she looks to the right so her face is in full profile with her Diana-inspired hair in a neat updo. She wears no jewelry except for a small, inconspicuous wedding ring on her left hand. She poses against a waist-high table in a dimly lit, grayish room. To the 21st century viewer without context, this is simply a large portrait of a pale woman in a black dress, but employing Max Baxandall’s concept of “the period eye” and T.J. Clark’s discipline of Social Art History, we can see how this subject and composition were overcome with scandal at the 1884 Paris Salon—so much scandal that Sargent fled France.

The scandal resulted from Sargent originally painting one of Mrs. Gautreau’s straps hanging down––a gesture charged with sexual innuendo that sparked moral outrage. The strong public reaction forced Sargent to repaint the strap to sit correctly on her shoulder. Sargent believed deeply that he had painted a masterpiece; however, the Salon, the public, the reviews, and even Mrs. Gautreau herself disagreed. The backlash prompted Sargent to flee Paris as his portraiture business was devastated by the events of the Salon.

Now lauded as Sargent’s ultimate masterpiece, Madame X was endlessly ridiculed by art critics and Parisian media. Madame X was kept in Sargent’s studio for nearly thirty years until the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York bought the painting in 1916 after Sargent pleaded the work’s case to the board. Contextualizing the social conditions surrounding the painting are critical to understanding the historical and artistic importance of Madame X and Sargent as a radical portrait artist of the late 19th century.

Around the year 1939, Edwin Fuerst designed the glassware “American Prestige” for Libbey Glass Company in Ohio. It arrived at a precarious time in history for both the company and the country at large (the latter of which was still recovering from the Great Depression). Indeed, just a few years prior in 1935, Libbey Glass Company was sold and became a subsidiary of Owens-Illinois Glass Company, due in part to their untimely expenditure on art glass (possibly the knickerbocker series) earlier in the decade during a period of heightened economic depression (1). Nevertheless, high-end glassware such as Fuerst’s “American Prestige” remained a part of the company’s production. The existence of such glassware, I argue, expresses a particular nationalist spirit entangled with American modern design through both style and discourse.

From a visual standpoint, it’s clear that the glassware can be categorized within the style of Art Deco, due to its rectangular stem and concentric base. Art Deco, a design movement associated with modernity and technological progress, is notable for its opulence (2). The name itself, “American Prestige,” conjures an association between the object’s luxurious style and the buyer’s wealth and power. But importantly this is not just about prestige in a general sense, but of a particular brand of prestige: that is, American.

Beyond just the objects’ style, there’s also the issue of how the company markets these objects, which reiterates this tendency toward a patriotic narrative. While difficult to trace advertisements that feature “American Prestige” specifically, I was able to find one of such advertisements in the December, 7, 1940 issue of the New Yorker. Two glasses, which appear to be the goblet and wine glass, are featured next to a large bowl, a set of candle sticks, and other decorative objects. In large font at the very top of the page, the advertisement displays the title “American Masterpieces" (3). In another Libbey advertisement that year (also in the New Yorker, though this time sans “American Prestige”), the tagline heralds the “Rebirth of American Supremacy in Crystal" (4). Thus, the significance of American national identity to the Libbey brand—and subsequently to “American Prestige”—is its appeal to American exceptionalism for the explicit sale of luxury commodities.
